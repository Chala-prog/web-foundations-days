# Day 8 Reflection

**What I learned.** The biggest lesson was that the average load tells you almost nothing. A normal day is about 6 requests per second, but the big sale is 5,000 or more, and the write attempts grow thousands of times. The system has to be designed for ten bad minutes, not for the other 23 hours. I also realised that only 20,000 of the 200,000 people can ever succeed, so most of the traffic is guaranteed to fail. The cheapest way to survive is to stop that traffic early (cache, waiting room, "sold out" flag) before it reaches the database.

**What was hardest.** Double-booking. At first I thought "check if the seat is free, then reserve it" was enough. Working it through showed me that two users can both pass the check before either one writes. The fix was to make the check and the write one atomic step (a conditional `UPDATE ... WHERE status = 'AVAILABLE'`), and to add a `UNIQUE` constraint on `tickets.seat_id` so that the database itself refuses a second ticket even if my code has a bug. Transactions give all-or-nothing, and constraints give a guarantee that does not depend on me remembering to write the right code.

**Trade-offs I had to accept.** Fairness and speed pull against each other: a waiting room makes some people wait, but it gives first-come, first-served and protects the database. Showing a slightly stale seat map is fine because correctness is decided at hold time, not on the screen. I chose one strongly consistent database over a fancier distributed one because the real number of successful sales (about 33 per second) is small.

**What I would improve.** I would load-test the waiting room and the hold query with realistic contention, add monitoring for hold-to-purchase conversion and for payments without tickets, and plan how to shard by event if the business grows. I would also reconsider the 10-minute hold length, since shorter holds free up seats faster during a big sale.
